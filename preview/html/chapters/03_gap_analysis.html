3. Gap analysis¶

The comparison uses the three proposed requirements defined in Chapter 2: permission attenuation (R1), agent identity and key binding (R2), and selective disclosure (R3). An illustrative two-level delegation flow is provided in Annex A.

This preliminary analysis follows the two complementary formats in ITU-T A Suppl. 6, clause 7 and Appendix I [P4]: an inventory of related work followed by identified gaps, proposed studies and related activities. The comparison is limited to the cited editions and public proposals reviewed on 14 November 2026. The gaps concern interoperable composition of R1-R3 across trust domains; they do not imply that the underlying mechanisms are unavailable or unstudied.

3.1. Existing standards and reusable mechanisms¶

Organization and reference

Relevant coverage

Remaining issue for this proposal

IETF: OAuth 2.0 Token Exchange, RFC 8693 [T1]; Standards Track RFC

Token exchange supports delegation and actor identification. Nested actor claims record previous actors.

Section 4.1 treats earlier actors as history, not access-control inputs. The RFC does not define a verifier-checkable permission-subset proof at every delegation step or selective disclosure of parent authority.

IETF: OAuth Rich Authorization Requests, RFC 9396 [T9]; Standards Track RFC

Structured, fine-grained authorization details for specific APIs and transactions.

Section 6.1 leaves comparison rules to each authorization type. Providers need agreed semantics for deciding whether a child grant is contained in its parent; JSON structure alone does not provide those rules.

IETF: OAuth DPoP, RFC 9449 [T2]; Standards Track RFC

Sender-constrained tokens and proof of key possession bind token use to an HTTP method and URI.

A DPoP key is not, by itself, a trusted agent identity or authority to delegate. Section 11.7 excludes general request-body integrity; the proposed profile also needs binding to relevant operation parameters and a delegation profile.

IETF: SD-JWT, RFC 9901 [T3]; Standards Track RFC

Selective disclosure of signed claims, with an optional holder-key-binding mechanism.

Selective disclosure does not establish a child’s authority under its parent or prove inequalities over hidden limits. An authorization profile must require key binding and identify claims or proofs essential to each decision, consistent with Section 9.7.

W3C: Verifiable Credentials Data Model v2.0 [T5]; Recommendation

Common issuer, holder, verifier and credential data model, usable with suitable securing mechanisms.

Section 5.9 explicitly requires an accompanying authorization framework. The data model alone does not define permission attenuation, authorized re-delegation or an agent-specific use proof.

These standards provide complementary building blocks. The issue is their composition: exchanging a token, proving a key and disclosing selected claims are separate operations. This proposal seeks common rules connecting an accepted root grant to each child agent and to the verifier’s decision under R1-R3. It does not propose replacing OAuth or the VC data model.

3.2. Related drafts and FG-TIDA proposals¶

Organization and reviewed work

Relevant coverage

Remaining issue for this proposal

IETF submission: Attenuating Authorization Tokens, draft -02 [T4]

Local attenuation, a defined tool-constraint vocabulary, holder-key binding and invocation proof of possession.

Section 10 acknowledges that chains can reveal task, tool, constraint and key-correlation information. Its chain-validation mechanism does not provide task-branch selective disclosure with proof of inherited constraints.

IETF submission: Verifiable Attenuated Delegation for AI Agent Chains, draft -03 [T7]

Parent commitments, deterministic attenuation checks, bounded depth and expiry, principal continuity and DPoP-based key possession.

Verification uses the signed tokens in the chain. The reviewed profile does not specify a selectively disclosed parent-evidence representation that hides unrelated permissions while preserving those checks.

IETF submission: OAuth 2.0 Delegated Authorization, draft -04 [T8]

An authorization server issues a key-bound root; clients locally sign constrained child tokens. The draft defines chain checks and type-specific permission containment.

Section 11 identifies information exposure when resource servers receive the full chain. The reviewed profile does not specify selectively disclosed parent evidence that preserves its chain and containment checks.

W3C: Data Integrity BBS Cryptosuites v1.0 [T6]; Candidate Recommendation Draft

Selective disclosure and unlinkable derived proof artifacts, including optional holder-binding features.

Deriving a presentation does not itself authorize a new agent or prove parent-child policy containment. An authorization profile and, for hidden numeric comparisons, an additional compatible proof mechanism are needed.

FG-TIDA public themes: ATHENA, issue #34 [F1]; Subject-Anchored Identity, Delegation and Discovery, issue #45 [F2]

Overlapping proposals address constrained delegation, agent binding and cross-domain verification; ATHENA also addresses privacy.

These theme descriptions do not specify the combined credential and disclosure profile studied here. The proposed work should contribute cross-domain delegation use cases and the R1-R3 analysis to these activities and identify opportunities to consolidate work.

The three Internet-Drafts are individual submissions and work in progress, not adopted IETF standards. The W3C BBS document is a draft rather than a Recommendation; the FG-TIDA issues are discussion proposals rather than approved ITU-T specifications. In particular, [T4], [T7] and [T8] already specify attenuation and key binding. Their overlap narrows the justification for this work to interoperable composition and privacy, rather than the invention of delegation itself.

3.3. Identified gaps and proposed studies¶

3.3.1. GAP-01 - Common attenuation semantics across service domains¶

Gap description. R1 needs different providers to interpret the same action, resource, audience, limit and delegation condition consistently. The reviewed drafts provide concrete containment rules within their respective vocabularies; a shared mapping across heterogeneous service domains is not established by those separate specifications. Hidden parent information must not make containment undecidable or let an applicable restriction be omitted.

Future work. Compare and reuse existing vocabularies and containment rules, identify only the missing mappings, and define how an implementation handles unsupported or undisclosed constraints. Use a bounded set of cross-domain task types, with Annex A as one illustrative flow.

Related work. RFC 8693 [T1]; Rich Authorization Requests [T9]; the delegation drafts [T4], [T7], [T8]; FG-TIDA themes [F1], [F2].

3.3.2. GAP-02 - Verifiable root authority linked to delegated agents¶

Gap description. R2 requires each delegation to link authorized agents and their keys. Verifiers also need a trusted basis for accepting root authority. Existing identity, key-possession and delegation mechanisms cover substantial parts of this chain; their composition needs explicit rules for root-authority trust, cross-domain identity mappings and operation binding, including when parent evidence is selectively disclosed. A valid signature alone cannot establish the principal’s rights at a service.

Future work. Specify the roles and trust evidence needed to accept a root grant, bind each grant to its designated agent, and bind execution to the intended operation. Reuse existing identity and PoP mechanisms; treat the user-signed root in Annex A as one issuance model.

Related work. DPoP [T2]; VC Data Model [T5]; delegation drafts [T4], [T7], [T8]; FG-TIDA themes [F1], [F2].

3.3.3. GAP-03 - Selective disclosure with verifiable attenuation¶

Gap description. R3 requires an agent to receive only relevant authorization information while the verifier still checks R1 and R2. The reviewed delegation drafts expose parent token contents needed for chain checks; SD-JWT and BBS provide disclosure mechanisms without defining delegated authority. Simply combining their formats does not establish that hidden constraints were respected or that a new recipient was authorized.

Future work. Study a delegation and presentation profile that hides unrelated task branches, preserves authenticated evidence of all applicable constraints, and binds that evidence to the child agent. Separately assess proofs over hidden parent values as an enhanced research objective. Compare disclosure and correlation properties before selecting a construction.

Related work. SD-JWT [T3]; BBS Cryptosuites [T6]; delegation drafts [T4], [T7], [T8]; ATHENA [F1].

These findings will inform the Technical Report described in Chapter 4, identifying reusable mechanisms, missing capabilities and opportunities to coordinate with related FG-TIDA work. The analysis does not select a credential format or claim that every gap requires a new standard.
